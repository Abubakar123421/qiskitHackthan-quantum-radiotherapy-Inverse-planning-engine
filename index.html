QURIE is a quantum planner for breast-cancer radiotherapy. It picks which beam angles to use so the target gets its full dose and the heart and lungs get as little as possible. It runs on IBM's Qiskit and was tested on the CT scans of 48 real patients.

How QURIE turns a treatment plan into a quantum problem

Four steps, from a patient's scan to a checked answer. No physics degree needed.

Step 1

Scan the patient

Doctors outline the target, the heart and the lungs on a CT scan. QURIE offers 12 possible beam directions around the treated side.

Step 2

Turn angles into switches

Each angle is a switch: on or off. 12 switches become 12 qubits. The rule: exactly 4 switches on, because machine time is limited.

Step 3

Let the circuit search

The quantum circuit holds every valid 4-beam plan at once. Cost layers push it toward low heart and lung dose; mixing layers swap one beam for another. Plans that break the rule never appear.

Step 4

Measure and check

Measuring the circuit 100 times gives a list of plans; the most common is usually the best. A normal computer then checks all 495 plans to prove it.

Tested on 48 real patients

25 left-breast and 23 right-breast patients from a public hospital dataset (Rezaei et al., 2017). The circuit's settings were tuned on one patient and reused, unchanged, on every other.

Honest limits

  • No quantum speed-up yet. At 12 angles a normal computer checks all 495 plans in about 2 milliseconds. That is exactly how we prove every quantum answer is correct.
  • Simulated, not on real hardware. The best circuit has about 2,000 two-qubit gates, too many for today's noisy quantum chips.
  • Simplified physics. One 2D slice per patient, straight beams, equal beam strengths. Real planning is 3D. Our dose numbers compare plans; they are not clinical doses.
  • Not a medical device. QURIE suggests angles for planners to review. It does not diagnose cancer or replace a physicist.
